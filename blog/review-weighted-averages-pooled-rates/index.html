Review Weighted Averages and Pooled Rates in AI Reports

An AI assistant can produce a tidy paragraph about an average while answering a different question from the one the table was meant to answer. The arithmetic may even be internally correct. If the assistant gives each branch, week or category equal influence when the intended unit is each underlying case, the resulting number describes a different population. Reviewing the calculation therefore starts with the thing being averaged and the reason for its weight, before it starts with a formula.

This guide develops original fictional examples for business readers preparing an AI-assisted report. The examples are deliberately small enough to reconstruct without an assistant. They are not client records, measured model outputs, benchmarks or recommendations for staffing, pricing or investment. The accompanying CSV contains the invented inputs, and the JSON contains manually specified exact expectations. No executable application is supplied. Arithmetic checks of those files establish agreement with those expectations; they do not establish that a real organization's definitions, records or decisions are sound.

Start with the question that one observation answers

Imagine two fictional review groups, Cedar and Maple. Cedar has eight completed checks out of ten eligible checks. Maple has twenty completed checks out of one hundred eligible checks. Their rates are eighty percent and twenty percent. A report writer asks for the overall completion rate across eligible checks. That question assigns the same influence to each eligible check, rather than the same influence to each group name. Pooling the counts gives twenty-eight completed out of one hundred ten eligible, or exactly fourteen fifty-fifths. Expressed as a percentage, that is approximately 25.45 percent.

A simple mean of the two group percentages gives fifty percent. That number is not an alternative rounding of 25.45 percent. It answers a different question: what is the mean of these two group rates when the groups receive equal weight? Both calculations have understandable definitions. Only the first matches the stated question about all eligible checks. An AI-written sentence saying that half the eligible checks were completed would therefore misdescribe these invented inputs, even though the assistant could correctly show eighty plus twenty divided by two.

Write the unit next to the question. Examples include one eligible check, one completed request, one branch, one day, one purchased item or one hour. Then write what would make two observations comparable. A completed request may have a duration, but an open request does not yet have a final completion duration. A branch percentage may describe ten cases or ten thousand. Without this unit statement, asking an assistant to calculate an average leaves a substantive reporting choice implicit.

Recover the total that the mean represents

For a count-weighted mean, each subgroup's mean can be converted back to a subgroup total by multiplying its mean by the number of observations behind it. Add those totals, then divide by the combined observation count. In fictional case B, Cedar has a mean handling duration of four minutes across two completed items. Maple has a mean of ten minutes across eight completed items. The implied totals are eight and eighty minutes. Together they describe eighty-eight minutes across ten completed items, giving a combined mean of 8.8 minutes.

The unweighted mean of four and ten is seven minutes. Again, seven is the mean of two subgroup means given equal influence. It does not reconstruct the total minutes in these ten fictional completed items. Notice that the larger group does not receive extra influence because it is more important politically, more reliable or more successful. It receives influence proportional to its item count because the question asks about the mean duration per completed item. A different question can require a different weighting rule.

This reconstruction also reveals a data limitation. If the subgroup means were already rounded for display, multiplying them by counts reconstructs only an approximation to the original total. Do not present the reconstructed value as exact merely because a calculator prints many digits. Ask for original totals or unrounded means when the distinction matters. Keep the rounding question separate from the population question: selecting the appropriate denominator does not recover precision that the source has already discarded.

Distinguish frequency weights from chosen importance

Case C uses the same two numerical values as a teaching exercise, but changes the meaning of the weights. Suppose a fictional scoring brief assigns sixty percent of a score to criterion Cedar and forty percent to criterion Maple. Their authored scores are four and ten. The resulting weighted score is 6.4 because four times 0.6 plus ten times 0.4 equals 6.4. These weights express a chosen scoring design. They are not counts of completed items, and the score must not be labeled the average handling duration from case B.

An assistant needs the type of weight as well as its value. A column headed weight might contain frequency counts, hours of exposure, percentages of a scoring rubric or values copied from an unrelated report. Matching the length of two columns is a useful mechanical check, but it does not prove that the columns belong together. Keep row identifiers when pairing values and weights. A sorted value column paired with an unsorted weight column can generate a plausible number from the wrong combinations.

For the original teaching calculations here, weights are nonnegative, the sum of weights must be positive, and each value has one explicitly paired weight. These are declared rules for these examples. They are not a universal specification for all statistical software or every legitimate analysis. If an actual workflow uses specialized survey, calibration or other statistical weights, the analytical purpose needs its own qualified explanation. A general AI summary should not silently replace that design with the elementary examples in this guide.

Pool rates only when their counts can be combined

The pooled-rate calculation in case A assumes that the two eligible groups describe compatible, nonoverlapping sets of checks under the same completion definition. That assumption is part of the authored example. It cannot be inferred just because two real table headings both say completion rate. One group could count an acknowledgment as completion while another requires an accepted result. One period could count newly received work while another includes old backlog. Adding those numerators would create a number with no stable meaning.

Overlap creates another problem. If one request appears in both a weekly total and a monthly total, adding those totals counts the request twice. A weighted formula does not correct duplicated membership. Before pooling, ask whether the rows are separate groups, cumulative snapshots, alternative classifications or overlapping views. Preserve any unknown answer in the report. It is better to provide separate rates with a clear limitation than to produce an overall rate from counts that cannot be combined on the available evidence.

The same caution applies to exposure measures. A count per hour, a count per item and a percentage of eligible cases have different denominators. They cannot be exchanged simply because all three can be written as decimals. Name the numerator and denominator in words before calculating. A useful AI brief requests an explicit calculation such as completed checks divided by eligible checks, followed by the counts used. That instruction makes a later review possible without relying on the assistant's choice of the word average.

Treat zero and missing as different situations

Fictional case D gives Cedar zero completed checks out of ten eligible checks, and Maple twenty out of one hundred. Cedar's zero is an observed value inside this invented dataset. Pooling gives twenty out of one hundred ten, exactly two elevenths, approximately 18.18 percent. Removing the zero-completion group would change the question and inflate the reported completion rate to twenty percent. Zero completed does not mean that the group has no eligible cases or should disappear from the denominator.

Case E instead gives Cedar a missing eligible count while retaining a completion count of eight. Maple still has twenty completed out of one hundred eligible. The overall pooled rate is unresolved under the declared rules because the total eligible count is unknown. Treating the missing value as zero would be a fabricated input. Excluding Cedar could yield Maple's known rate, but that result must be labeled as the Maple subset. It is not the overall rate for both named groups.

A third situation is zero eligible cases. A rate with a zero denominator is undefined for the ordinary division used here. Do not automatically display zero percent and then average it with other percentages. Explain that the subgroup has no defined rate under this calculation. If its numerator is positive despite a zero eligible count, the pair also requires a definition or data correction before interpretation. These distinctions matter more than choosing a visually consistent number of decimal places in a dashboard.

Changing the mix can change the overall story

Fictional case F contains two groups across two invented periods. In period one, Cedar completes nine out of ten checks and Maple completes twenty out of one hundred. In period two, Cedar completes eighty out of one hundred and Maple completes one out of ten. Cedar's rate falls from ninety to eighty percent. Maple's falls from twenty to ten percent. Yet the pooled rate rises from twenty-nine out of one hundred ten to eighty-one out of one hundred ten, approximately 26.36 to 73.64 percent.

There is no arithmetic contradiction. The second period places far more eligible checks in the group with the higher rate. The group mix changes while the within-group rates decline. These authored numbers illustrate why an overall trend does not by itself establish that each component improved. They do not explain a real business change or identify a cause. A report should show both subgroup rates, both subgroup denominators and both pooled totals before using a broad word such as improvement.

Equal total denominators across periods do not prevent this effect. Both fictional periods contain one hundred ten eligible checks. The composition inside that total changes from ten Cedar and one hundred Maple to one hundred Cedar and ten Maple. An assistant asked only to compare the pooled percentages could omit this important context without making an arithmetic error. Ask it to compare the weights as well as the final results, and to distinguish a descriptive aggregate change from evidence about component performance.

Keep a stable comparison separate from the observed aggregate

A reader may want to know what happens if both periods use the same group weights. That is a different, explicitly constructed comparison. For example, assign equal weight to Cedar and Maple in each period. Their simple mean rates then move from fifty-five percent to forty-five percent. This chosen comparison answers how the mean of two equally weighted group rates changed. It does not replace the observed pooled rates of the invented cases, and it must not be labeled as the percentage of all eligible checks.

The point is not that equal group weights are always better. A report can need both the actual pooled description and a standardized comparison, each with a clear purpose. The weights for a constructed comparison should be stated before interpreting the result, along with the reason they were selected. Quietly changing weights until a preferred story appears makes the output hard to assess. An AI assistant should expose that choice rather than hide it in a polished explanation.

Keep causal language out of this arithmetic exercise. The original case does not identify why group volumes changed, whether the groups handled comparable work, whether eligibility rules stayed fixed in practice or whether any intervention occurred. The authored assumptions make the fractions calculable; they do not create evidence for operational causes. A useful sentence describes the mix and the rates directly. A stronger conclusion needs information beyond the six teaching cases and beyond the assistant's confidence in its own wording.

Give an assistant a bounded calculation brief

A practical original brief might read: Use only the supplied rows. Define the reporting unit as one eligible check. Keep Cedar and Maple separate while calculating their rates. For an overall rate, add compatible completed counts and divide by the sum of compatible eligible counts. Do not average displayed percentages for that overall question. Preserve missing counts as missing. Show the numerator, denominator, exact fraction and a separately labeled approximate percentage. State any unresolved compatibility or overlap assumption before writing the summary.

For a mean-duration task, replace that brief with the correct totals and unit. Ask the assistant to multiply each unrounded subgroup mean by its completed-item count, sum the implied durations, and divide by the completed-item total. Require a note if only rounded subgroup means are available. Do not reuse the pooled-completion instruction merely because both tasks contain a division. The numerator of a mean duration is accumulated time, while the numerator of a completion rate is a count of completed checks.

The brief can also request a small result structure: question, unit, included rows, excluded rows with reasons, value-weight pairs, numerator total, denominator total, exact result, display result and limitations. This is an original teaching structure, not an observed model response or an established company policy. It makes omissions easier to notice. It does not make the assistant a trusted calculator, authorize changes to a reporting system or establish that sensitive data can be sent to a particular tool.

Review the explanation against the underlying rows

Start review with one row and follow it through the calculation. In case A, the eight completed checks and ten eligible checks must stay paired. In case B, four minutes must stay paired with two completed items. A final result inside the range of subgroup values is a useful plausibility check for these nonnegative weighted examples, but it is not proof. Both seven and 8.8 lie between four and ten. Only one reconstructs the mean duration of the ten authored completed items.

Then check a limiting case. If the two subgroup rates are identical, their pooled rate will match that common rate when compatible positive denominators are used. If one subgroup has no eligible cases, do not invent a rate for it. If all weights are multiplied by the same positive factor, a weighted mean should remain unchanged. These checks can expose formula or pairing mistakes in a teaching calculation. They do not validate the collection process, missingness assumptions or the fitness of a real dataset for a management decision.

Finally, read the prose without looking at the number. Does it say overall when the calculation covers a subset? Does it describe a branch average as a case average? Does it turn a higher pooled rate into a claim that every group improved? Does it imply that a missing denominator was confirmed? The language can overreach even after the arithmetic is corrected. Preserve the calculation boundary in the sentence that a reader will actually see, including the counts and the chosen reporting unit.

Frequently asked questions

Is averaging percentages always wrong?

No. An equal-weight mean of subgroup percentages answers a legitimate question when each subgroup is intended to receive equal influence. It differs from the overall rate across underlying eligible cases when group denominators differ. State which question is being answered. For the overall completion question in case A, the appropriate authored calculation uses twenty-eight completed out of one hundred ten eligible checks. The equal-group result of fifty percent should carry its own label instead of replacing that pooled result.

Can I recover a pooled rate from percentages alone?

Usually the subgroup denominators are also needed. Eighty percent based on ten eligible checks contributes differently from eighty percent based on a thousand. Rounded percentages can also hide original counts. If denominators are missing, preserve the limitation instead of assigning equal denominators by default. An equal-weight average may be calculable from the displayed percentages, but it answers the equal-group question. It does not reveal the number of completed checks in an unknown combined population.

Do weights have to add to one?

For the elementary weighted mean used here, nonnegative weights can be counts or proportional values, and the positive total normalizes them. Two and eight give the same weighted mean as 0.2 and 0.8 when paired with the same values. The meaning of those weights still matters. Counts of completed items describe a different question from deliberately assigned scoring importance. This answer is limited to these teaching calculations and does not specify rules for specialized weighting methods or every software interface.

Why can an overall rate rise when both group rates fall?

The relative group sizes can change. Case F moves more eligible checks into Cedar, the higher-rate group, in the second invented period. Both within-group rates decline while the pooled rate increases. Show group counts and rates alongside the pooled totals to make that composition visible. The example demonstrates an arithmetic possibility. It does not establish the cause of a real trend, show that any operating intervention worked or justify a forecast about the next period.

Should a missing count be entered as zero?

Only if zero is the actual supported value under the dataset's definition. A missing eligible count means the denominator is unknown; zero eligible cases means something different and gives an undefined subgroup rate under ordinary division. Case E leaves the overall result unresolved. A report may describe the known Maple subset with an explicit scope label. It must not present that subset result as the overall rate for both groups or imply that an absent value was verified.

Do the downloadable checks establish that an AI calculation is reliable?

They establish agreement between these authored files and these exact arithmetic expectations. They do not test a language model, inspect a production report or validate a real collection process. A new dataset can introduce overlap, changed definitions, rounded inputs or missing membership information that the fictional cases do not resolve. Use the files to practice a reviewable explanation, then assess the authorized data and reporting question separately before relying on a real result.
